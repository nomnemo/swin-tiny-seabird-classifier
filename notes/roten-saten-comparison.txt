  So yes — the cropped version is worse across the board and learned slower. Here's why:

  1. The "context leak" was actually helping, not hurting. With only 4 source aerial photos and 1,270 total images, the model had very limited data.
  The full 256x256 images gave it extra signal — background texture, neighboring bird density, vegetation patterns — that happened to correlate with
  species in this small dataset. Removing that context took away useful (if spurious) features, leaving the model with less to work with.

  2. The birds are very small and low-resolution. The median bbox is only ~65x46 pixels. After cropping with 25% padding, you're stretching a ~80x58
  pixel patch up to 224x224. That's heavy upscaling of already-blurry aerial imagery, which destroys fine-grained detail rather than revealing it.
  The original 256x256 images at least preserved the native resolution.

  3. More variance, same data size. The cropped images vary more in what the model sees (just the bird from different angles/poses), making the task
  genuinely harder per-sample. With only ~500 training images per class, there isn't enough data to learn the bird-only features robustly — hence the
   much noisier validation curve (bouncing between 0.70-0.91) compared to the smoother original run.

  4. ROTEN recall dropped the most (96.9% → 84.4% on test). Royal Terns are larger birds, so they likely benefited more from the surrounding context
  in the full images. Once cropped, the model loses that size-in-context cue.

  Bottom line: Cropping is the right approach in principle for fine-grained classification, but it needs higher-resolution source imagery to work.
  With these aerial photos where the birds are only ~50-70px across, the bbox crops are too low-res after upscaling. The full masked images, despite
  the confounding context, give the model more usable pixel information. For this dataset, the original approach (full 256x256 masked) is the better
  choice.
